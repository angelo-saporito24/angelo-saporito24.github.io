2026 Working paper

Partially Identified: privacy suppression and the automation–augmentation measure in the Anthropic Economic Index

The Anthropic Economic Index reports, for each O*NET task, how much observed AI usage is automation rather than augmentation. The measure has been adopted quickly: it underpins occupation-level wage regressions, an NBER working paper, and regional exposure indices, and the index itself is cited in Federal Reserve Board staff research. I show that the measure is partially identified. To protect privacy, the index suppresses any cell holding fewer than 15 conversations from fewer than five accounts, which censors the data on two margins: modes within observed tasks, and whole tasks within occupations.

Suppression removes only 6.38% of classified conversation volume, but it concentrates in thin cells, and most tasks are thin. Two errors in a naive reconstruction of the published automation share nearly cancel, one biasing it up 0.55 points and the other down 0.47, so a pipeline that has failed twice appears to validate.

2026 Working paper

Belief-State Structure in Reinforcement Learning for Monetary Policy

Sole-authored. Extends two LSE group projects, in deep learning and reinforcement learning, that I proposed and led.

What happens to a learned policy rule when the central bank observes a forecast rather than the state? I train PPO and SAC agents in a three-equation New Keynesian economy in which the bank sees only a belief supplied by a forecaster-informed noise model, then project the policy they learn onto the Taylor rule. In all twelve full-budget runs the long-run inflation response falls to between 0.011 and 0.025, and none is distinguishable from zero.

The size of the collapse is consistent with attenuation under signal noise about five times the simulated dispersion of inflation. The design can’t yet separate that from a learning failure, since agents that see the state also fall short of the analytical rule; doing so needs the optimal policy under partial observation as a comparison. The contrast the paper set out to measure, calibrated against overconfident beliefs, is not identified, and is reported that way.

2026 MSc capstone

Modelling Tail Risk and Asymmetry in Energy Spread Trading

With two other students, in partnership with a European energy-trading firm.

We forecast the extreme quantiles of the spread between day-ahead and real-time electricity prices at ERCOT’s four major hubs, using CAViaR models extended with grid fundamentals and estimated under a strict walk-forward protocol: 13,872 monthly fits over seventeen delivery months. Statistical and economic evaluation came apart. The most accurate specification beat the baseline on pinball loss in all 48 of its 2025 comparisons, but lost its calibration in the 2026 holdout while the plain baselines kept theirs, and the exposure rule built on the forecasts traded profit for lower volatility and drawdown. The evidence supports a monitored risk-control input, not a profit engine.

2026 Course paper

Ambiguity as Evidence: probabilistic preprocessing and topic uncertainty quantification in Ancient Greek philosophical texts

Bayesian Machine Learning, LSE.

Ancient Greek is highly inflected, and deterministic lemmatisation discards the ambiguity of its word forms before any model sees the text. I treat morphological analysis as probabilistic inference and propagate the uncertainty through topic models of twelve works of Plato and Aristotle. A matched control then showed that the topic model’s own initialisation noise reproduces 98.8% of the headline uncertainty measure; morphology alone accounts for a smaller but real 28.6%, against 36.9% for the confounded figure. What remains is structured, and varies with rhetorical register even where corpus size is fixed by construction, against the view that ambiguity is mainly a problem of corpus size.
