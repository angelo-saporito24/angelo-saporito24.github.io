2026 Revised essay (2022)

Private Government and the Public Constitution of Authority: Anderson on Liberalism and the Firm

Elizabeth Anderson argues that the firm is a government, one that exercises arbitrary and unaccountable authority over the people who work in it. I read her genealogy of market egalitarianism alongside John Dewey, then argue that the standard defence of workplace authority, that workers consent to it and remain free to leave, fails because the relation it calls private is constituted in public law. The essay ends by asking whether a workplace constitution can reach a power that works by managing conduct rather than by command.

2026 Revised essay (2022)

The Entrepreneurial State and the Public Origins of Private Innovation

A sequel to the essay on Anderson.

This essay extends the argument from authority to wealth. With Mariana Mazzucato and Karl Polanyi, I argue that where innovation is most uncertain the State is often the principal investor, so that much of what is celebrated as private invention rests on publicly funded foundations, and that the harder question is who is rewarded. Calling the private capture of public investment parasitic needs a theory of value that market prices can’t supply, and the book assumes one more than it provides it.

2026 Revised essay (2022)

Alienation through Liberation: Freedom, the State, and the Alienated Individual

Patrick Deneen argues that liberalism failed because it succeeded. I develop a distinction he draws but leaves undeveloped, between liberty as the absence of constraint and freedom as purposive activity within a whole one recognises as one’s own, and read his account of the liberal state through the young Hegel’s diagnosis of Rome. The mature Hegel then sets the test for Deneen’s remedy: whether local communities can restore purpose without asking the modern individual to give up her claim to direct her own life.

2026 Revised essay (2023)

Some Metaphysical Considerations Concerning Pragmatism: Language, Ontology, and Morality

Pragmatism, unusually, has a datable beginning, in the Metaphysical Club of the early 1870s. I follow its accounts of language, ontology, and morality from Peirce and James to Rorty and Brandom, and argue that it reorients the tradition from Plato to Kant rather than rejecting it, along a path Hegel first took: philosophy as a practice carried on within history. Brandom’s expressive freedom then explains how moral reform works from inside a community’s norms.
